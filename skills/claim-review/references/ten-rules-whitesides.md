# Ten structural rules and the Whitesides outline method

Use this reference to apply the structural critical review. Evaluate each rule at the supplied document's maturity: inspect a completed section when present; otherwise inspect its explicit structural plan. Mark evidence that cannot yet exist as `unknown` or `not applicable`, not automatically `fail`.

For a story arc, apply the rules to the central theme, subthemes, and logical progression. Assess the functions of context, evidence, and conclusion without requiring manuscript sections or paragraph plans; sectioning belongs to the outline stage.

## Rule-by-rule structural review

### 1. One central contribution

**Test**

- State the paper's contribution in one sentence without joining unrelated claims with “and.”
- Check that the candidate title communicates the contribution, not merely the topic or method.
- Check that every main figure is necessary for this contribution.

**Failure signals**

- Several competing headline claims.
- A title that names a system but not the discovery.
- Results that are interesting yet logically unrelated.

### 2. Write for a real, uninformed reader

**Test**

- Ask an independent reader to state the question, answer, and importance after reading only the title, abstract or structured abstract plan, and figures.
- Check that terms are defined before use and that abbreviations are necessary.
- Identify knowledge the argument assumes but never supplies.

**Failure signals**

- The story depends on the reader knowing the project's chronology.
- Specialist terms conceal the causal or physical idea.
- Too many unresolved threads must be remembered simultaneously.

### 3. Use context-content-conclusion

**Test**

- At paper scale: introduction supplies context, results supply content, and discussion supplies the bounded conclusion.
- At section or paragraph scale: orient the reader, present evidence or reasoning, and state the resulting conclusion.
- At figure-sequence scale: explain why a test is needed, show the test, and state what it establishes.

**Failure signals**

- Results appear before the motivating question.
- Evidence is presented without explaining its evidentiary role.
- Paragraphs or figures end without a conclusion.

### 4. Maintain linear logical flow

**Test**

- Place related evidence and parallel comparisons together.
- Use the same term for the same concept.
- Draw the dependency chain between result conclusions; check for backward references or missing premises.

**Failure signals**

- Repeated topic switching.
- A concept is introduced, abandoned, and resumed later.
- Synonyms make one mechanism appear to be several mechanisms.

### 5. Tell a complete abstract story

**Test**

- Find the broad context, specific gap, approach, principal results, bounded answer, and broader relevance.
- Check that the stated answer is no stronger than the evidence.
- For an early outline, require a structured abstract plan containing these roles rather than polished prose.

**Failure signals**

- Background dominates while the answer is vague.
- The abstract reports activities rather than conclusions.
- Significance appears without the gap or supporting result.

### 6. Establish why the unresolved gap matters

**Test**

- Move from broad field importance to the precise unknown addressed here.
- Explain why resolving that unknown changes understanding, prediction, or capability.
- Ensure the final introduction paragraph previews how the work addresses the gap.

**Failure signals**

- A broad literature survey without a precise unknown.
- Novelty is asserted only as “not previously studied.”
- The promised gap differs from the one the results actually address.

### 7. Deliver results as a claim chain supported by figures

**Test**

- Give each result section and main figure a declarative conclusion.
- Link every conclusion to visible quantitative evidence and required controls.
- Check that later conclusions depend only on earlier established results.
- Include credible alternatives at the point where the evidence discriminates between them.

**Failure signals**

- Chronological experiment reporting.
- Figure titles describe procedures rather than conclusions.
- The decisive control is deferred or absent.
- Several figures repeat one observable without adding independent support.

### 8. Explain resolution, limitations, and relevance

**Test**

- State exactly how the original gap was filled.
- Separate supported conclusions from interpretation and future speculation.
- Name limitations and resulting nonclaims.
- Explain what new question, prediction, or conceptual connection becomes possible.

**Failure signals**

- The discussion simply repeats results.
- Limitations are generic or missing.
- Generality expands beyond tested systems without an argument.

### 9. Allocate effort to high-leverage elements

**Test**

- Check that the outline, title, structured abstract plan, figure order, and conclusion-style figure titles exist before extensive prose polishing.
- Verify that each planned paragraph or figure has one explicit evidentiary role.
- Confirm that missing research is driven by a claim gap or credible objection.

**Failure signals**

- Polished paragraphs surround an unresolved central claim.
- Large supplements compensate for an unclear main argument.
- Additional work has no stated claim or objection it will settle.

### 10. Iterate through skeptical feedback

**Test**

- Seek at least one naive-reader pass and one scientifically skeptical pass.
- Record what each reviewer understood as the central contribution.
- Track major objections and whether they require evidence, a narrower claim, clearer logic, or no action.

**Failure signals**

- Feedback is limited to grammar or general approval.
- The author cannot explain the outline in a few minutes.
- Major revisions accumulate without reconsidering the story architecture.

## Whitesides outline protocol

Treat the outline as the working scientific product from which prose will later be assembled.

1. List the objectives, hypotheses, observations, equations, figures, and candidate conclusions.
2. Organize the data into an argument rather than the chronology of the research.
3. Place each figure and result where it establishes a necessary conclusion.
4. Iterate on the outline until collaborators agree on the data, logic, and claim boundaries.
5. Use unresolved outline disagreements to identify missing research or overstrong interpretation.
6. Begin substantial prose only after the evidence-and-logic structure is coherent.

Do not interpret “agree” as suppressing dissent. Record unresolved alternative interpretations and state what evidence would discriminate among them.

## Combined stopping question

Before adding a result, paragraph, or simulation, ask:

> Which central claim, logical dependency, or credible objection will this settle?

Classify work that does not settle one of these as optional unless it is required for transparency or reproducibility.
