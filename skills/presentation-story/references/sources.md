# Sources and scope

This is locally authored guidance for planning the storyline of a scientific talk. It is not an official skill or endorsement from the authors below. Sources are paraphrased; presentation advice is treated as expert guidance rather than experimentally established rules.

## What each source contributes

- **William C. Ratcliff:** [The David Attenborough Style of Scientific Presentation](https://math.nyu.edu/inmemoriam/donev/Teaching/WrittenOral/David-Attenborough.pdf), two-page guide, full text inspected. Adopted: the three phases (setting up, methods and results, concluding); opening with big-picture context and zooming in to the key questions; asking questions early that the talk answers later; stating the main result early instead of building suspense; brief methods; a conclusion that recaps the answers and returns to the big picture; transitions as the most common place to lose the audience; revealing only what the current point needs; anticipated questions placed after the acknowledgements; conclusions not displaced by a long acknowledgement list. The related article is [The science of storytelling: the David Attenborough style of scientific presentation](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molmed.2023.05.002), Trends in Molecular Medicine 29(9), 675–677 (2023); its [bibliographic record and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37479552/) were verified, and the journal full text was not accessible during source review.
- **Arjun Raj:** [Some thoughts on how to structure a talk](https://rajlaboratory.blogspot.com/2016/09/some-thoughts-on-how-to-structure-talk.html) (2016), full text inspected. Adopted: a talk is a story with a narrative arc, unlike a paper; an early contrast between what the field thinks now and what the talk will show, returned to at the end; an introduction that ends with a major result; the audience as the one making the journey, with the best talks answering the question the listener has just formed; a middle that adds supporting evidence and addresses alternatives; a turn toward what the results imply; reminders of key concepts and results because listeners miss parts of every talk; a recurring visual (credited to McConnell) shown at intervals; no heavy framework up front. Raj credits Nancy Duarte's *Resonate* (2010) and the Pixar story formula for the arc. Dramatic contrast must not exaggerate the scientific contribution; that limit is a local addition. His [2014 presentation tips](https://rajlaboratory.blogspot.com/2014/01/simple-tips-to-improve-your.html?m=0) credit Uri Alon for speaker-led explanation, sentence titles, and sparse slide text.
- **Susan McConnell:** [Designing Effective Scientific Presentations](https://www.ibiology.org/professional-development/scientific-presentations/), iBiology, recorded 2010; transcript inspected. Adopted: a middle built from bounded episodes that start at a general level, go into the data, and come back up with a summary; a home slide (one familiar image shown at each transition to recap what was shown and preview what comes next); the minimum data needed to convince; agreement between what is displayed and what is said; a clearly signalled conclusion that returns to the opening questions, without false endings.
- **David L. Stern:** [A Talk Is Not a Paper](https://www.howtogiveatalk.com/blog/the-structure-of-an-effective-talk), [Tell and Show](https://www.howtogiveatalk.com/blog/principle-7-tell-and-show), [Don't Take a Data Dump on Your Audience](https://www.howtogiveatalk.com/blog/principle-5-dont-take-a-data-dump-on-your-audience), and [Finish When You Are Done, Preferably Sooner](https://www.howtogiveatalk.com/blog/principle-8-finish-when-you-are-done-preferably-sooner); full text of all four inspected. Adopted: breadth over depth; a plain, concrete opening problem; methods only to the depth needed to trust the work; the decisive experiment explained with care while other supporting experiments are mentioned and offered for discussion; each result explained in more than one way; going into detail and then returning to a conclusion everyone can follow; introducing the question before showing the data, then explaining the axes and the result; one result at a time; one or two major points, three at most; speaking for about two-thirds of the allotted time.
- **Blake Snyder:** *Save the Cat! The Last Book on Screenwriting You'll Ever Need* (Michael Wiese Productions, 2005). The term "beat sheet" and the idea of drawing a story as a curve across three acts come from screenwriting practice popularized by this book. The local diagram is original: its beats are the stages of a scientific talk from the sources above, and its vertical axis is the level of detail, not dramatic tension. Snyder's fifteen beats are not used.
- **The `claim-review` skill:** researcher ownership of the interpretation, agreement between claims and evidence, and logical progression. Its manuscript drafts, critical-review modes, and team-review requirements are not dependencies of this workflow.

## Where the sources disagree

- **Slide titles:** Alon (through Raj), McConnell, and Ratcliff recommend a sentence that states each slide's point. Stern's [advice against slide text](https://www.howtogiveatalk.com/blog/principle-1-dont-put-words-on-slides) removes titles so the audience reads the data first. The beat sheet does not decide slide titles; it heads each sequence with a question and ends it with a take-home statement, which either title style can use at the slide stage.
- **Audience:** Ratcliff and McConnell tailor the level of detail to the audience. Stern prepares for a general scientific audience and assumes no knowledge of the field. The local choice is to ask who the audience is while planning and to explain goals and methods in plain language either way.
- **Written signposts:** McConnell recaps at each transition with a home slide. Stern avoids agenda slides and relies on spoken introductions. The local choice is a home slide without an agenda list.
- **Formal conclusion:** Ratcliff and McConnell always recap. Stern treats a formal conclusion as optional when the audience is engaged. The local choice is always to answer the opening questions.

## Local adaptations and exclusions

- Question headings, take-home statements, the flexible number of beats, the `talk.md` format, and the placement of the level of detail on the diagram's vertical axis are local adaptations requested by the maintainer.
- Rehearsal, eye contact, pointers, humour, colour and font choices, and formal question-and-answer coaching are outside this skill. Slide construction is a later stage described in [the Slidev handoff](slidev-handoff.md).
