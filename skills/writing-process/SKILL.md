# Writing Process

Start with the author's material and the task they want to finish. If they have an outline and ask for a Results paragraph, draft that paragraph. If they ask where to begin, identify the next writing decision and help them make it. Use the [19 writing lessons](references/lessons.md) to guide that work.

## Choose the starting point

| What the author brings | What to work on now |
| --- | --- |
| Loose notes, a mind map, or an opening they keep rewriting | Order the ideas, expand the whole outline one level at a time, and give each planned paragraph a purpose. For a perspective or essay, use ideas and supporting arguments as the outline levels. Use lessons 1–6. |
| Results or figures for a primary data paper | Use lesson 7 to choose a drafting stage. Work from the figures already available. If the titles are settled, use them as Results headings and draft from the corresponding panels. Use lessons 8–13. |
| A draft with an unclear beginning or ending, excess length, or ideas that depend on each other | Find the passage where the reader loses the argument. Revise it, suggest a specific cut, or briefly introduce interdependent concepts before returning to their details. Use lessons 14–19. |

## Work with the author

For a primary data paper, Michael Levin's staged writing sequence is (1) story, outline, and working Abstract, (2) figures, starting with their titles, (3) Results, (4) Methods and Acknowledgments, (5) Discussion, and (6) Introduction. Follow the author's requested stage and build on their completed work. A figure title states one supported finding. A Results subsection can use several figures to answer one question.

Use the author's scientific story and the evidence they supply. If the proposed title asserts a mechanism but the figure establishes only a difference between conditions, write a title for that difference. Identify the unresolved question if it matters to the next paragraph. Preserve missing statistics, citations, or contributor details as explicit gaps. Ask for additional information only where it changes what can be written.

Return something the author can use in the manuscript, such as ordered paragraph points, figure titles, section prose, or a specific cut with its reason. When useful, name the next writing action. At the end of a full draft, compare the working Abstract with the finished Results and Discussion, correct any claims that have changed, and apply the venue's length limit.

## Use a slide visual when it helps the current lesson

The lesson guide pairs seven diagrams with the writing decisions they illustrate. Use lessons 5–6 when moving from a mind map to an outline and from outline points to paragraphs, lesson 7 when choosing drafting order, lessons 9–10 when turning figure titles into Results, lesson 14 when checking section shapes, and lesson 17 when introducing interdependent ideas.

When a diagram helps, link to the corresponding slide in the lesson guide and explain what to look for in the author's material. When the author simply needs prose or a cut, deliver it directly.
