# Writing Process

- Start with the author's material and the task they want to finish.
- **If they have an outline and ask for a Results paragraph:** draft that paragraph.
- **If they ask where to begin:** identify the next writing decision and help them make it.
- Use the [19 writing lessons](references/lessons.md) to guide that work.
- **If `story_arc.md` or a `claim-review` review exists:** build on its claims, their reviewed scope, and its figure roles; do not rewrite `story_arc.md`.

## Choose the starting point

| What the author brings | What to work on now |
| --- | --- |
| Loose notes, a mind map, or an opening they keep rewriting | Order the ideas, expand the whole outline one level at a time, and give each planned paragraph a purpose. For a perspective or essay, use ideas and supporting arguments as the outline levels. Use lessons 1–6. |
| Results or figures for a primary data paper | Use lesson 7 to choose a drafting stage. Work from the figures already available. If the titles are settled, use them as Results headings and draft from the corresponding panels. Use lessons 8–13. |
| A draft with an unclear beginning or ending, excess length, or ideas that depend on each other | Find the passage where the reader loses the argument. Revise it, suggest a specific cut, or briefly introduce interdependent concepts before returning to their details. Use lessons 14–19. |

## Work with the author

- **For a primary data paper:** Michael Levin's staged writing sequence is (1) story, outline, and working Abstract, (2) figures, starting with their titles, (3) Results, (4) Methods and Acknowledgments, (5) Discussion, and (6) Introduction.
- Follow the author's requested stage and build on their completed work.
- A figure title states one supported finding; a Results subsection can use several figures to answer one question.
- Use the author's scientific story and the evidence they supply.
- **If the proposed title asserts a mechanism but the figure establishes only a difference between conditions:** write a title for that difference.
- Identify the unresolved question if it matters to the next paragraph.
- Preserve missing statistics, citations, or contributor details as explicit gaps.
- Ask for additional information only where it changes what can be written.
- Return something the author can use in the manuscript, such as ordered paragraph points, figure titles, section prose, or a specific cut with its reason.
- When useful, name the next writing action.
- **At the end of a full draft:** compare the working Abstract with the finished Results and Discussion, correct any claims that have changed, and apply the venue's length limit.
- Once the requested writing step is delivered, stop; do not draft further sections unasked.

## Build the paragraph-level outline

- Own `outline.md`: create it from the [outline template](assets/outline-template.md) or revise it in place, preserving the author's material.
- Turn the confirmed story arc, or the author's confirmed argument, into the chosen manuscript sections in order; narrative steps need not map one-to-one to sections.
- Write one bullet per planned paragraph stating its main point, with the supporting calculation, simulation, or measurement on the same bullet where useful.
- Place each figure at its point in the argument with its number, a bounded conclusion-style title, its source file or planned status, and a draft caption covering panels, quantities, conditions, units, and statistical definitions; mark unknown details `UNKNOWN`.
- Keep planning notes, claim checks, and open decisions in the template's supporting-notes sections, not in the manuscript outline.
- **Early outline:** may include hypotheses and planned work, labeled as such. **Pre-draft outline:** contains completed results and bounded claims only.

## Call on Claim Review only when needed

- Call `claim-review` when a claim appears that was not reviewed, when a title, sentence, or outline point is worded more strongly than its reviewed status, or when the underlying evidence changed.
- If no review exists, proceed with the author's claims and label them unreviewed; do not run a review unasked.

## Use a slide visual when it helps the current lesson

- The lesson guide pairs seven diagrams with the writing decisions they illustrate:
  - lessons 5–6 when moving from a mind map to an outline and from outline points to paragraphs;
  - lesson 7 when choosing drafting order;
  - lessons 9–10 when turning figure titles into Results;
  - lesson 14 when checking section shapes;
  - lesson 17 when introducing interdependent ideas.
- **When a diagram helps:** link to the corresponding slide in the lesson guide and explain what to look for in the author's material.
- **When the author simply needs prose or a cut:** deliver it directly.
