# Writing lessons

These lessons adapt Michael Levin's personal writing method. Use the outlining lessons for a research paper, perspective, or essay. The six drafting stages apply to a primary data paper. [Levin's essay, *How I write papers*](https://thoughtforms.life/how-i-write-papers/) · [Companion slides](https://thoughtforms.life/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/add-to-Substack-post.pdf)

Selected lessons link directly to the corresponding diagram in Levin's companion slides.

## From ideas to paragraphs

1. **Start with an outline.** List the ideas a reader needs to understand your finding. While those ideas are still bullets, you can move one earlier or drop it without having to rewrite a paragraph.

2. **Expand the whole outline one level at a time.** Order the main ideas across the paper. Then add the subsections each idea needs. Once the subsections are in place, add paragraph points across all of them. For a perspective or essay, start with the ideas you want readers to consider and expand each into its supporting arguments.

3. **Find the decision behind a repeated rewrite.** If you keep rewriting the opening because a later result changes what the reader needs to know, return to the outline. Identify the missing idea or move the result. Once the sequence makes sense, rewrite the affected passage.

4. **Decide what to say before working on wording.** During the creative phase, choose what belongs, what can wait, how the ideas relate, and what the reader needs to learn first. Stay with brief points until the whole outline reaches paragraph level. During drafting, turn those points into sentences and add the numbers, statistics, and figure references each needs.

5. **Put connected ideas in an order a reader can follow.** A mind map can connect a question, a control, and an interpretation in several directions. Choose where a reader can enter. For example, establish what is known and where the gap lies, introduce the system and the experiment with its controls, then explain the answer and its implications. Give each planned paragraph one job in that sequence.

   [Companion slide (page 5): A network of connected ideas becomes an ordered outline of the puzzle, the approach, and the answer.](https://thoughtforms.life/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/add-to-Substack-post.pdf#page=5)

6. **Turn one outline point into one paragraph.** Pick a paragraph point, explain it in sentences, and supply the evidence it needs. Repeat with the other points. If the paragraph depends on something the reader has yet to learn, put that missing step in the outline and resume drafting.

   [Companion slide (page 6): Arrows connect the lowest-level points in the outline to the paragraphs that develop them.](https://thoughtforms.life/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/add-to-Substack-post.pdf#page=6)

## Drafting a primary data paper

7. **Choose a writing order that lets one section prepare the next.** Readers meet the Introduction early. You can write it last, once you know exactly which question the paper answers. In Levin's sequence, the outline and working Abstract guide the figures, the figure titles guide the Results, and the completed findings guide the Introduction. Use the six stages below to find your next writing task.

   [Companion slide (page 7): Two rows compare reading and drafting order. The Introduction comes second for the reader and sixth for the writer.](https://thoughtforms.life/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/add-to-Substack-post.pdf#page=7)

8. **Stage 1 · Settle the story and write a working Abstract.** Decide which findings belong in this paper and outline how to present them. In roughly 250 words, introduce the problem, describe the approach, state what you found, and end with what the finding means in that broader context.

9. **Stage 2 · Write the figure titles, then build the figures.** Give each result figure one major finding and state it in the title using a verb. A descriptive title suits an opening schematic. Read the titles alone to see whether a colleague could follow the argument. In Levin's example, they lead from an assay through a treatment effect, a proposed mechanism, and a rescue experiment. Review your sequence with your PI or collaborators while the titles are easy to change. Then build each figure with the panels needed to support its title.

   [Companion slide (page 9): Four hypothetical figure titles move from an assay schematic to a treatment effect, a proposed mechanism, and a rescue experiment.](https://thoughtforms.life/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/add-to-Substack-post.pdf#page=9)

10. **Stage 3 · Draft the Results from the figure titles.** Give each Results subsection a heading that closely matches the finding in the corresponding figure titles. A subsection can draw on several figures. Start with the question and the approach you took. Describe the experiment and what you observed, pointing to the figure and statistics. End with the conclusion those observations support.

    [Companion slide (page 10): A Results subsection develops a question through evidence to a conclusion. The example matches a figure title to the corresponding Results heading.](https://thoughtforms.life/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/add-to-Substack-post.pdf#page=10)

11. **Stage 4 · Write Methods and Acknowledgments.** Describe every technique another group would need to repeat the work. Pay particular attention to a step that took your lab several attempts to get right. Give the reader the detail that made it work. In Acknowledgments, name the people who helped and identify the funding sources.

12. **Stage 5 · Explain what the findings mean in the Discussion.** Recap the findings, using a summary figure or model if it helps connect them. Explain what was puzzling or hard to see in the data alone. Show where the result agrees with, contradicts, extends, or overturns earlier work. Give limitations and future work their own subsections. In the first, identify what you left untested and what remains uncertain. In the second, explain what researchers can now do. Close with a possibility the findings open for the field.

13. **Stage 6 · Write the Introduction last.** Start with the field and the prior work that establishes what is known. Narrow to the gap and explain why a reader would want it resolved. Identify the part your study addresses and how. End with a sentence or two that previews the principal findings and claims. Cite the work that establishes this framing.

## Revising the draft

14. **Check how the Introduction, Discussion, and Abstract move.** Read the first and last sentences of each section. The Introduction should narrow from the field to the question. The Discussion should widen from the findings to their implications. Give the Abstract an hourglass shape by moving from the broader problem to the specific approach and result, then back out to the meaning of the finding.

    [Companion slide (page 14): The Introduction narrows, the Discussion widens, and their two shapes combine into the hourglass of the Abstract.](https://thoughtforms.life/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/add-to-Substack-post.pdf#page=14)

15. **Give readers a citation trail they can follow.** Cite the earlier work that lets a reader trace an idea to its origins. Keep the sources in a reference manager such as EndNote. Ask someone familiar with the field to read the draft for overlooked work or contributors. AI can also suggest leads to check. If a journal caps citations, keep the fuller reference list in the preprint.

16. **Cut for purpose once the draft exists.** Set word counts aside while drafting from the outline. On revision, ask of each paragraph whether it takes the reader where the paper is heading, what it contributes to the main theme, and what effect it has on the reader. Cut the material that serves no purpose. A preprint can carry a fuller treatment.

17. **Introduce interdependent ideas in a spiral.** When understanding A requires B and understanding B also requires A, introduce both briefly before explaining either in full. With four concepts, give readers a short first pass through A, B, C, and D. Then revisit each in the same order with the details. Add another pass if readers need more context to understand the next idea.

    [Companion slide (page 17): A spiral introduces a, b, c, and d on its inner turn, then returns to A, B, C, and D on the outer turn.](https://thoughtforms.life/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/add-to-Substack-post.pdf#page=17)

18. **Use the lessons to find where a draft needs work.** If the argument keeps changing, return to the outline, expand it by level, and settle the order before polishing. If the Results are hard to follow, review the figure titles with collaborators and trace each subsection from question through evidence to conclusion. Once the findings are clear, finish the Introduction, check the citations, and cut paragraphs that have lost their purpose. Where readers need several concepts at once, introduce them in successive passes.

19. **Keep what works for you.** If you already have a productive writing routine, use the lesson that helps with the task in front of you. An author with a settled outline and finished figures can start directly with the Results.
